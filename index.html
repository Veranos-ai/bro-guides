FAQ

Common Questions

Quick answers to what newcomers ask most, each with a link to the full resource.

What Is Reconstitution?

Reconstitution means mixing a dry (freeze-dried) powder with a liquid, usually bacteriostatic water, so it can be drawn up. It is the first step most researchers describe when a vial arrives as a powder. Read the Mixing Guide →

How Do Researchers Work Out the Amounts on a Syringe?

The numbers come from three things: the mass in the vial, the volume of water added, and the target amount. Dividing mass by volume gives the strength, and the target divided by the strength gives the draw volume. The calculator does this and shows each step. Open the Reconstitution Calculator →

How Do I Read the Marks on a Syringe?

Most researchers use a U-100 insulin syringe, where 100 units equal 1 mL. The guide shows the barrel, the marks, and how to read them against the draw volume. Read the Syringe Guide →

How Long Will a Vial Last?

That depends on the vial size and how much is used each week. The supply calculator adds up several vials and shows the weeks and months they cover, so a study can be planned around them. Open the Supply Calculator →

How Do Researchers Usually Store Peptides?

Researchers commonly move vials to the fridge once they arrive, and keep mixed vials in the fridge too, taking them out only when needed. A mixed vial is commonly kept for about 30 days. The guide has the full table for powder and mixed vials. Read the Storage Guide →

How Do Pre-Filled Pens Fit In?

Some researchers receive a pen that is already filled, and others fill a cartridge from a vial they mixed themselves. The pen guide describes the parts and the two routes, and points to the maker's leaflet for use. Read the Pen Guide →

What Do the Words on the Vial and Data Sheet Mean?

The glossary explains the technical terms used on this site in a sentence or two, such as lyophilised, diluent, aliquot and COA. Open the Glossary →

Where Do the Findings on This Site Come From?

Every finding carries a label: cell study, animal study, human study, review, or common practice. The labels are explained near the top of this page. See the Labels →
